# Five Indian-subsidiary filings Singapore regional finance teams miss

The Indian subsidiary of a Singapore group runs on India's April to March year, whatever the parent's year-end. Most of its filings sit in the monthly routine and get done. The five below do not, because nothing in the routine prompts them. They are the filings I would check first if I were reviewing an Indian subsidiary from Singapore.

This is general information on Indian law. Singapore tax, IRAS and ACRA filings, and Singapore company law, stay with your Singapore adviser. I work on the Indian side only.

## 1. FC-GPR after a further allotment

**Answer first:** FC-GPR is due within 30 days of the date the shares are allotted to the Singapore parent, not within 30 days of the money arriving.

Group treasury tends to treat the remittance as the event. The Reserve Bank's clock runs from allotment, under regulation 4(1) of the FEMA non-debt instruments regulations. There is also an earlier deadline: the shares must be allotted within 60 days of receiving the money, or the money has to be refunded within the next 15 days. At the outside, that gives about 90 days from remittance to a completed report.

The usual miss is not the first investment at set-up, which everyone plans for. It is the second or third capital injection, years later, made to cover a funding gap. If FC-GPR is late, the filing can still be regularised through a late submission fee for up to three years. After that it becomes a contravention that has to be compounded.

## 2. The FLA return

**Answer first:** the annual FLA return is due on 15 July every year, even when no money moved during the year.

It is a statement of position, not a report of a transaction. A subsidiary files it if foreign investment is outstanding at the end of March of the reporting year or of the year before. It falls in July, usually before the Indian audit is signed, which is why it slips. The RBI's FAQs allow provisional or unaudited figures, with a revised return once the accounts are final. A company that has only received share application money, with nothing allotted and no other foreign investment outstanding, does not file.

Ask your Indian team who owns this date, and whether the shareholding and any loans or trade credit with the Singapore parent have been confirmed to them before July.

## 3. A transfer pricing report for a cost recharge alone

**Answer first:** any international transaction with an associated enterprise needs the accountant's transfer pricing report, whatever its value.

The report is Form 3CEB for financial year 2025-26, due by 31 October 2026, with the income tax return by 30 November 2026. From tax year 2026-27 it becomes Form 48 under section 172 of the Income-tax Act, 2025, first due by 31 October 2027.

The common miss is the subsidiary that does not think of itself as trading with the parent. It only reimburses the parent's costs, or pays a share of group software. Those are still international transactions with an associated enterprise, and there is no value threshold. If the report is not filed, the return cannot be completed correctly. The parent's group transfer pricing team should confirm the intercompany prices that go into the report each year.

## 4. DIR-3 KYC for directors who live in Singapore

**Answer first:** DIR-3 KYC now comes round once every three financial years, and the form depends on the director's personal mobile number and email.

For a director based in Singapore, those details may have changed since the last filing, and the verification codes go to them. The filing is due by 30 June for directors whose three-year cycle falls due. A change in a director's mobile number, email or address has to be filed within 30 days of the change. A late or reactivation filing costs Rs 5,000 in government fees.

A simple control: ask every Singapore-based director to tell the Indian company when their contact details change, and keep a record of each director's next KYC year.

## 5. TDS on payments to the parent

**Answer first:** each payment to the Singapore parent should be checked for Indian tax before it is made, even if it is only a cost recharge.

Not every payment is taxable in India. But the check has to happen. Where tax is deductible under section 393(2) of the Income-tax Act, 2025 (section 195 of the 1961 Act), it is deposited by the 7th of the next month, the remittance is reported in Form 145, with a Chartered Accountant's certificate in Form 146 where the rules require one, and the payments go into the quarterly Form 144. Interest on late deposit runs under section 398.

The miss happens when intercompany balances are netted or settled by group treasury and never pass through the Indian payables run. A treaty rate is a ceiling, not an automatic result. The India-Singapore treaty caps Indian tax at 10% on royalties and technical fees where the Singapore company is the beneficial owner, but the Indian payer needs the IRAS Certificate of Residence and Form 41 (Form 10F for payments in financial year 2025-26 and earlier) to apply it. The Singapore parent obtains the certificate from IRAS itself.

## A short control list

- Put the allotment date, not the remittance date, in the group calendar.
- Name an owner for 15 July.
- Ask whether any intercompany charge exists, however small, before the transfer pricing season.
- Record each director's KYC year.
- Route every payment to the parent through the Indian TDS check.
